## Identity

- Accepted name *Epinephelus itajara* (Lichtenstein, 1822); original combination *Serranus itajara* (WoRMS).
- English names used on fetched pages: Atlantic goliath grouper (NOAA); goliath grouper (Florida Museum, FishBase); historically “jewfish” (NOAA, WoRMS vernaculars).
- WoRMS also lists marine, brackish, and (on the taxdetails page) a freshwater/terrestrial environment flag. The species-profile pages describe **marine and brackish** use. This profile does not treat the species as a freshwater product.

## Range

**Western Atlantic (consistent across NOAA, Florida Museum, FishBase, WoRMS notes):**
- NOAA: Gulf of America (NOAA’s current name for the Gulf of Mexico) and Florida Keys; Bahamas; most of the Caribbean; most of the Brazilian coast. Occasional records off New England (Massachusetts and Maine).
- Florida Museum: Florida south to Brazil, including the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean.
- FishBase: Florida, USA to southern Brazil, including the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean; discrete western-Atlantic populations (Ref. 89627).
- WoRMS distribution note: Chesapeake Bay, Virginia and possibly north to Massachusetts, to Brazil, including Bermuda and the West Indies; throughout the Gulf of Mexico.

**Eastern Atlantic:**
- NOAA, Florida Museum, FishBase: Senegal to Congo; FishBase and Florida Museum add rare in the Canary Islands.

**Eastern Pacific — do not collapse with *E. itajara*:**
- Florida Museum still writes “also present in the eastern Pacific from the Gulf of California to Peru.”
- FishBase: “Eastern Pacific (Gulf of California to Peru) species refers to *Epinephelus quinquefasciatus*.”
- This slice treats **Atlantic** goliath grouper only. Pacific records are out of taxon unless a later source with rights review says otherwise.

**Modeling implication:** a global heatmap of “goliath” would mix taxa and basins. The first domain is southwest Florida, not the whole FAO range.

Fetched pages agree on **shallow, structured, often inshore** water. They do not give a live depth for any individual.

## Life history (only numbers the pages stated)

From Florida Museum unless noted:

- Largest Atlantic grouper / sea-bass on these pages. NOAA and Florida Museum: up to **8 feet** and **800 pounds**. FishBase: max length **250 cm TL**; max published weight **455.0 kg**; common length 150 cm TL.
- Oldest **verifiable** age on Florida Museum: **37 years**; that page *projects* perhaps 50 years — the 50-year figure is marked there as a projection, not a measured specimen. FishBase max reported age: **37 years**. NOAA: lifespan **more than 30 years**.
- Florida Museum maturity: males **4–6 years**, **43–45 in (110–115 cm)**; females **6–7 years**, **47–53 in (120–135 cm)**.
- Growth on Florida Museum: about **4 in (10 cm)/year** to age 6; about **1.2 in (3 cm)/year** at age 15; **<0.4 in (1 cm)/year** after 25.
- FishBase: length at first maturity **128.0 cm**; generation time **8.7 years** (model estimate on that page); resilience low (doubling time 4.5–14 years).
- Hermaphroditism: Florida Museum says most groupers are protogynous but **goliath sex change is still debated**; a 1992 study found no transitional individuals. Largest individuals are described as invariably male on that page. **Do not model a confirmed sex-change schedule from this profile.**
- Diet (Florida Museum / FishBase): crustaceans (especially spiny lobster), fishes (including stingrays), octopus, young sea turtles.
- Adults: solitary; limited home ranges; little inter-reef movement (FishBase). Territorial threat display and swim-bladder rumble (Florida Museum).
- Larvae: pelagic; Florida Museum: transform to benthic juveniles at about **1 inch (2.5 cm)**, around **25 or 26 days** after hatching.

## Spawning (season only — no site coordinates)

Florida Museum (accessed 2026-09-22): spawning in **July, August, and September** throughout the range; lunar-influenced; offshore aggregations of **up to 100 or more** individuals on shipwrecks, rock ledges, and isolated patch reefs. The same page says 1980s aggregations fell to **fewer than 10** per site, and after protection rose to **20–40** per location. Those counts are historical statements on that page, not a current survey.

NOAA: offshore adult work each **summer/fall spawning period since 1994**; tagging and observations of aggregations in the Gulf and, later, the South Atlantic. NOAA names the Ten Thousand Islands as the **juvenile** study area (1997–2005), not as a list of aggregation wrecks.

**Do not publish wreck names, lat/lon, or dive-site pins.** A seasonal window is not a live aggregation map.

## Modeling implications (honest)

1. Ontogeny is two habitats: mangrove/estuary juveniles vs structure-oriented adults. A single surface layer will confuse them.
2. Adults have small home ranges most of the year and **seasonal aggregation** in Jul–Sep. A 24–72h product is not the same as a seasonal product.
3. Harvest bans and rare legal state permits change **observation effort**. Catch is not abundance; diver reports cluster on wrecks.
4. Aggregation and nursery coordinates are **sensitive**. Public grain must stay coarse (~1° or larger) or withheld.
5. Habitat (mangrove, reef, wreck, 0–50 m) is **suitability language**, never presence.
6. No page fetched here supplies a calibrated P(present | lon, lat, depth, time). **Unknown is the correct issued state** until gates pass.
