## Candidate 1 — *Magallana gigas* (Pacific oyster), Washington farms

### Life cycle (farmed)

NOAA Fisheries (species page, updated 2026-04-13) and FAO cultured-species notes agree on the broad sequence:

1. **Protandrous hermaphrodite.** First-year animals typically function as males; some later become female when food and temperature allow; sex can reverse if food is poor (FAO).
2. **Broadcast spawning.** Females may release ~50–200 million eggs (NOAA; FAO 50–200 million for 8–15 cm females). Gametogenesis begins near **10°C** at salinities ~15–32. **Spawning generally requires ~18–22°C** (often cited >20°C; regional variation). In cooler WA basins, animals may ripen without a full spawn.
3. **Planktonic larvae ~2–3 weeks** (NOAA: ~20–22 days to settlement). This is the **ocean-acidification / low Ω_aragonite** bottleneck (2009 WA hatchery “seed crisis,” Pacific Shellfish Institute report to WDFW).
4. **Settlement as spat** on shell, rock, or gear; thereafter **sessile**.
5. **Grow-out 18–30 months** to market under good conditions (FAO); WA intertidal vs off-bottom culture changes time-to-market.
6. **Farm pathways:** hatchery larvae → remote set → seed → planted bags/bottom/longline/raft. Natural set still matters on Willapa reserves and some fattening beds (DNR lease language still describes cultch and natural set).

**Wedge implication:** 24–72 h operational stress is about **spat through market adults already on the farm**, plus **gear**. It is not a larval-dispersal model and not a wild-stock recruit model.

### Seasonality in Washington (operational, not harvest-authorization)

| Season | Biology | Operator-relevant 24–72 h hazards |
|--------|---------|-----------------------------------|
| **Winter** | Slow metabolism; low spawning risk | Storms, freezing air on extreme lows, freshwater floods, gear icing |
| **Spring** | Rising temperature; gametogenesis | Freshwater runoff, turbidity, first handling of seed, early HABs |
| **Summer (peak)** | Spawning window; “summer mortality” in literature; highest water/air heat | **Heat × low tide**, hypoxia on neap tides, HABs, handling of ripe stock, crowding |
| **Early fall** | Post-spawn condition low | Residual heat, first autumn storms, lingering hypoxia |

**Documented WA/Salish event timing**

- **Late June 2021:** atmospheric heat dome **coincident with the lowest tides of the year** (26–28 June). Mass intertidal mortality; Pacific oysters worse than Olympia oysters; some deaths delayed days–weeks (Raymond et al. 2022 *Ecology*; WA Sea Grant Rapid Response Network). This is the template 24–72 h event.
- **Summer 2018–2019:** WDFW compiled grower reports of 80–90% bag mortality in Discovery Bay and northern Whidbey Basin; Willapa bottom culture also hit; hypothesized heat, low oxygen, nutrition, *Protoceratium*/yessotoxins; disease not confirmed in 2018 sampling.
- **Cheney et al. 2000:** 1998 Puget Sound summer mortality associated with **high temperature, neap tides, low oxygen**, not a single diagnosed pathogen.
- **Hatchery larvae:** summer upwelling can import low-pH, high-CO2, high-salinity water (2009 PSI/WDFW seed crisis). Different operator (hatchery) and different stage.

**Culture-method seasonality**

- Intertidal bags: summer **daytime lows** are the danger window (hours, not months).
- Subtidal/raft: water-column heat and DO; less aerial heat, more hypoxia/HAB exposure.
- Bottom culture in Willapa: summer growth/food (chlorophyll, residence time) is a **weeks-to-months** process (fattening line), not 72 h mortality — unless a heat/hypoxia/storm event.

**What seasonality does *not* justify**

- Using NSSP seasonal “conditional” rainfall closures as oyster stress (those are fecal-coliform harvest rules).
- Using satellite bloom calendars as mortality without a named HAB mechanism.
- Predicting spawn hour from a moon table (temperature/degree-days dominate).

## Candidate 2 — *Oncorhynchus tshawytscha* (Chinook), CA/OR ocean charters

### Life cycle (anadromous, diverse)

NOAA Fisheries (species and protected pages):

1. **Spawning in freshwater** (summer–fall for most CA/OR ocean-relevant stocks; winter-run Sacramento is a distinct, **ESA-endangered** life history).
2. **Juvenile rearing** in streams, floodplains, estuaries. Ocean-type (subyearling) vs stream-type (yearling) life histories.
3. **Smolt ocean entry** — CA Central Valley and Klamath fish dominate early-ocean surveys off CA–OR in some years (Hassrick et al. 2016); they stay **near natal outlets, shallow, high chlorophyll** for weeks–months.
4. **Ocean feeding 1–5+ years.** Adults/subadults piscivorous (forage fish, squid). Archival tags: persistent **8–12°C** thermal habitat, depth adjusted seasonally (deeper in winter and when surface is anomalously warm) (Hinke et al. 2004, 2005).
5. **Return and death after spawning** (semelparous).

**Wedge implication:** the charter’s 24–48 h question is stage (4) **legal ocean fish**, not (3) juveniles. JSOES and NWFSC stoplight indicators are built for **early-ocean survival of juveniles** and **adult returns 1–2 years later**. Using them as tomorrow’s bite is a stage error.

### Seasonality for CA/OR ocean sport Chinook

**Biological / fishery calendar (typical, not a 2026 regulation reprint)**

| Period | What is generally true | 24–48 h meaning |
|--------|------------------------|-----------------|
| **Winter–early spring** | Few legal ocean sport opportunities in many CA areas; some OR ports have winter Chinook in some years | Model should output **undefined / closed**, not a habitat score masquerading as fish |
| **April–May** | Monterey-area CA sport often the first CA opening in years that have a season (2026 Monterey opened 11 April per CDFW). Fish still mixed-stock. | Thermal habitat + weather; stock mix not identifiable at 48 h |
| **June–August** | Core charter season south of Cape Falcon when open. ODFW weekly tables show strong port and year differences (e.g. 2026 vs 2025 cumulative Chinook). CA areas have **harvest guidelines** and in-season closure. | Highest data density for CPUE evaluation |
| **Late summer–fall** | Increasing proximity of some stocks to natal rivers (GSI: Central Valley CPUE patterns shift); Klamath-area constraints; special river-mouth closures (Smith, Klamath, Eel). | **Do not** produce mouth-specific “hotspots” for listed stocks |
| **Multi-year** | PFMC abundance forecasts (Sacramento fall, Klamath fall, etc.) set **how many days even exist**. 2023–2024 CA ocean sport largely closed; 2026 reopened with guidelines. | Cohort size is a **seasonal prior**, not a 48 h map |

**Oceanographic seasonality (habitat, not count)**

- **Upwelling season** (roughly spring–early fall): cold, high-nutrient, high-chlorophyll nearshore bands; also hypoxia at depth on the shelf. Adults may sit at the temperature/DO intersection, not at the greenest satellite pixel.
- **Relaxation / marine heatwave:** surface too warm → tags show **deeper** fish (Hinke et al. 2005, August 2003). A 48 h product that only uses SST can **invert** the encounter signal (surface “bad” while mooching deep still works).
- **Juvenile June surveys** (WA/OR): not the charter calendar in CA.

**Run / stock seasonality (do not map listed ESUs)**

- Central Valley **fall** Chinook: usually the bulk of CA sport catch when fisheries are open.
- Klamath fall: management constraint for CA/OR KMZ.
- Sacramento **winter-run** and CA Coastal Chinook: ESA; ocean impacts are managed with harvest-rate proxies, not public fine-scale locations.

### What seasonality does *not* justify

- “Spring salmon” as a separate species.
- Treating weekly ODFW catch as a 48 h nowcast without weather and effort.
- Using moon phase as a primary Chinook ocean predictor (unverified at this horizon).

## Candidate 3 — *Homarus americanus* (American lobster), Gulf of Maine

### Life cycle

NOAA Fisheries (updated 2026-05-22) and ASMFC 2025 overview:

1. **Mating after female molt;** sperm stored up to ~1 year.
2. **Eggs** 5,000–>100,000; carried **9–11 months**; hatch inshore late spring–early summer.
3. **Pelagic larvae**, four molts, then settle. Larval duration shortens in warmer water; food (*Calanus*) and match–mismatch affect survival (ASMFC 2025).
4. **Benthic juveniles** need shelter (rock, cobble, burrow). Settlement surveys in shallow traditional nurseries; deeper settlement is an open research question as GOM warms.
5. **Many molts** (~20–25) over **5–8 years** to legal size (NOAA). Temperature increases molt frequency and can reduce size at maturity.
6. **Adults** territorial; coastal animals use rocky (or mud) shelter; larger animals move more; offshore animals may migrate tens to >100 miles seasonally.
7. **Longevity** potentially decades (age hard because of molt).

**Wedge implication:** next-trip CPUE is stage (5–6) **legal benthic catchability**, not larval settlement. Settlement and VTS sublegals inform **next years**, except that **this year’s molt pulse** suddenly adds legal recruits.

### Seasonality in inshore GOM (Maine statistical areas 511–513)

| Period | Biology | Fishery / CPUE |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| **Winter–early spring** | Cold bottom water; low activity; some offshore/deep distribution | Low inshore CPUE; Mills et al. 2017: spring landings rates low until temperature/molt cues |
| **Late spring** | Approach of ~5–6°C molt-initiation threshold; egg hatch | Timing of “start” of inshore fishery year is **forecastable at seasonal scale** from April temperature (Mills et al. 2017), **not** as a 6-hour nowcast |
| **Summer** | Molt, inshore movement, high feeding | Rapid rise in legal CPUE; 80%+ of some fleet landings associated with recently legal, post-molt animals (cited in Mills et al. from ASMFC) |
| **Fall** | Still high catchability in many inshore areas | Peak landings months in Maine monthly tables |
| **Following winter** | Activity drops | Effort allocation shifts deeper/offshore for some |

Maine DMR VTS samples **June–August** specifically to index juveniles in the warm season. Sea sampling is May–November plus winter trips.

**2025 assessment environmental seasonality (stock, not trip)**

- GOM inshore thermal habitat still generally **favorable** for growth/settlement vs SNE (where >20°C stress is common).
- GOM/GBK abundance down **34% from 2018 peak** but not depleted; **overfishing** in the 2025 determination — a **multi-year** exploitation issue, not a next-trip covariate.
- *Calanus* decline since mid-2000s: **recruitment**, 5–8 year lag to legal size.

### Catchability vs abundance through the year

This is the seasonal fact the model must keep visible:

- Summer CPUE up can mean **more legal recruits + higher catchability**, not a stock explosion that week.
- Spring CPUE down can mean **cold, pre-molt, inactive** animals still present.
- Survey catchability itself is temperature-dependent (ASMFC 2025 assessment uses temperature covariates).

## Cross-walk: which biological calendars belong in a v1 feature set

| Calendar feature | C1 oyster 24–72 h | C2 Chinook 24–48 h | C3 lobster next trip |
|------------------|-------------------|--------------------|----------------------|
| Hourly tide × solar noon | **Yes** (aerial heat) | No (except mouth closures) | Weak |
| 24–72 h weather / wind | Yes (storms, air T) | **Yes** (go/no-go, catchability) | **Yes** |
| Water/bottom temperature anomaly | Yes (water + air) | Yes (thermal habitat, **not** count) | **Yes** (catchability) |
| Chlorophyll climatology | Growth only | Juvenile literature only | No |
| Spawn/molt phenology | Summer mortality confounder | Run timing as prior | **Molt pulse** as prior |
| Regulatory season | Out of scope (food safety) | **Must-have mask** | License/zone/season mask |
| Multi-year stock assessment | No | Seasonal prior only | Seasonal prior only |

**Rule:** if a process cannot move in 24–72 h (cohort size, settlement year class, NSSP sanitary survey, ESA listing status), it is at most a **prior or a mask**, never the predicted layer.
